not a lot, just forever

Yuna sees the headline before she gets the call.

Boston Raiders’ Star Center Ilya Rozanov Traded to Ottawa Centaurs.

It’s a miracle her phone doesn’t crack from how hard it hits the ground. 

Once she’s over her initial shock, her brain barrels into confusion. She’s been a hockey fan for as long as she can remember. She grew up loyal to Ottawa, despite their tendency to lose. Like, every time. The first time she even considered sporting another team’s merch, was when Shane was drafted by the Metros, and even then, she still followed the Centaurs religiously, as if some day, some young upstart rookie would get drafted, and finally move them out of the last spot in the rankings. 

Never in her life did she expect Ilya Rozanov to be their saving grace. 

So, when the call from Shane comes, she only has one question. 

“Why would he do that?” She says, a little harsher than she meant for it to sound. 

“I need you to have an open mind with what I’m about to tell you,” Shane replies. She doesn’t miss the fear in his tone, but she doesn’t ignore it, either. 

“Always a great way to start,” she says, sarcastically, hoping it will get him to loosen up. He’s so tense all the time, and as a mother, it’s hard not to pry. 

“Mom, please,” he says, his tone bordering on desperate. “I’ll explain everything tomorrow, I swear, but I really need you to be okay with this.” His voice nearly breaks at the end of his plea, and her stomach twists. 

“Shane, honey, what happened?” 

Shane inhales sharply, his breath hitching. “That house next door to you? The one that was just sold?” 

“What about it?” Yuna asks, unable to tell where the conversation is going. Usually, Shane is one of the easiest people to read on planet earth. He wears his heart on his sleeve, even though he typically doesn’t intend to, and it’s one of her favorite things about him. Now, though, she’s at a loss, and she finds that she kind of hates the feeling. 

“Your new neighbor is Ilya Rozanov.” 

He says it like a talk show host would announce a surprise guest, right before they burst out of a giant cardboard cake. The silence that follows is potent, as Yuna tries to work out whether or not she’s hallucinating their entire conversation. 

“I’m sorry?” She asks, sure she’s only misheard him. 

“Ilya Rozanov is moving into the house next to you. Tomorrow. Around noon, I think.” It comes out in bursts, and Shane still somehow seems out of breath when he’s done. “I’ll have to double check his flight info,” he adds. As if he’s not talking about Ilya fucking Rozanov. As if he’s not casually working his archrival’s plane touching down into their family’s shared Google calendar. 

Something she’s learned when it comes to Shane, though, is that when he sounds as anxious about something as he appears to right now, it’s best to trust that he has meticulously planned how the entire thing will eventually play out for at least three days in advance. Usually, a spreadsheet or two get involved, as well. It’s just how he works, and Yuna wouldn’t change it one bit. 

So, when her son tells her that Ilya Rozanov needs her help moving his stuff into the house next door to hers, she doesn’t protest. 

She asks if he needs someone to pick him up from the airport (no, he insisted on getting an Uber), if he will be hungry for lunch by then (yes, mom, he’s always hungry), and if he’s allergic to anything (no, but if she asks him, he’ll say that Shane’s green smoothies will give him a rash). She jots everything down on a notepad that she digs out of the bottom of a drawer in her office, the Ottawa Centaurs logo stamped at the top. A list of groceries she needs to buy in order to make Ilya Rozanov’s favorite meal sits below it, begging her to pry. 

She thinks about how quickly Shane responded when she asked about Rozanov’s allergies, and decides that whatever it is that’s going on between the two of them has clearly been going on much longer than anyone could have guessed. Anything she wants to know will still be true tomorrow, when she is apparently due to move Ilya Rozanov into his new house next door. 

She hangs up the phone, wasting no time before getting up to find her husband. 

“Was that Shane?” David asks, barely looking up from the newspaper crossword he’s been working on since breakfast. 

“Yes, and he had some updates about lunch tomorrow,” she replies, sliding into the seat across from him. 

David puts his pen down, and looks up expectantly. 

“We’re going to have a guest joining us.” He nods, patiently waiting for her to elaborate. “That guest is Ilya Rozanov.” 

David, bless his soul, is clearly doing everything in his power to not appear too alarmed. 

“And,” Yuna continues, because she’s about as good at delivering news as her son is, “he will also need help moving into his new house here in Ottawa.” 

Nodding, David goes to open his mouth, but Yuna beats him to it. 

“The one next door, yes.” 

“Shane called to tell you all of that?” David asks, sounding much calmer than Yuna knows he really is. He’s always been better at presenting a cool exterior, but they’ve been together long enough for her to know that his brain is working overtime to make sense of the whole thing. 

“That, as well as his plane’s ETA,” she sighs, unable to keep herself from smiling a bit. 

Their son loves fiercely; like he doesn’t know how to do anything but. She doesn’t know who Ilya Rozanov is to Shane, or vice versa, but she’s been in love for long enough to know that it isn’t always loud and in your face. Often, the most intimate acts of love are quiet. In Yuna’s opinion, the best way to be loved is to be known. 

And, thanks to Shane, she knows that Ilya Rozanov’s plane is landing at Ottawa International at 11:37 AM tomorrow, and that he has very strong opinions about fruit on pizza. 

—

The doorbell rings at 12:15 on the dot, and Yuna tries not to trip over herself as she rushes to get the door. She knows very well that Shane has his own copy of their housekey, and there’s only one other person she’s expecting today, so she reaches for the doorknob, takes a deep breath in, and reminds herself that it only has to be a big deal if she makes it one. 

When the door opens, she’s met with a sight she never thought she’d live to see. Standing on her doorstep is an incredibly nervous looking Ilya Rozanov, a carry-on sized suitcase in one hand, and a bouquet of bright pink tulips in the other. Yuna feels her mouth open, but no words are coming out. 

“Shane says they are your favorite,” Rozanov says, holding the bouquet out for her to take. His accent is thicker than she remembers it being on TV, which she hopes is a good thing. 

She’s no stranger to the animosity that comes with having a foreign accent in a new country. She can’t imagine it’s been easy for him, especially with the amount of questions that are constantly being hurled at him after games. Hopefully, here, he’ll understand that Yuna could care less how heavy his accent is. Hell, if he needs her to, she’ll learn Russian just so that he can voice his needs in a language he feels comfortable using. 

It’s bold, she knows, to consider learning a new language for someone you barely know. But Rozanov brought her a bouquet of tulips because Shane told him that they’re her favorite flower, and she has a feeling that he won’t be someone she barely knows for much longer. Really, she hopes he can become someone she knows very well. 

“You didn’t have to bring anything,” she tells him, because she feels like he doesn’t actually know that. Something about the way he stands makes her think that he hasn’t gone many places where he’s been allowed to show up with nothing to offer but himself, and have that be enough. “I appreciate you thinking of me, though,” she tacks on with a warm smile. Taking the bouquet, she steps back to allow him in, and he enters the house cautiously. 

She watches as Rozanov dutifully steps out of his sneakers, and places them neatly on the rack by the door, without her having to ask him to. The action seems so intrinsic to him, that it catches her a bit off guard. Shane’s taught him well, she thinks. 

“Thank you for doing that,” she acknowledges, before nodding in the direction of the kitchen. “If you’d like, you can follow me, and we can put these in some water, then get started on lunch?” 

Rozanov nods, his hands shoved deep in his pockets like he doesn’t know what else to do with them. He’s so tense, and it makes Yuna’s heart hurt a little. She wonders how many places he has to go where he doesn’t have to have his guard up so high. Someday, she hopes their home can be one of those places.

They file into the kitchen, and Yuna passes him the bouquet to hold onto while she searches for a vase to put it in. It’s silent between them, but it’s not awkward, and to her, that’s a start. She fills the vase with water, and moves to take the flowers from Rozanov, who she observes has carefully extracted them from the paper they came wrapped up in. Once the flowers are soaking, and the vase has been placed in the middle of the kitchen island, it’s time for them to start making lunch. 

“I asked Shane,” she says, reaching into the fridge to grab the ingredients she’d bought yesterday. “He said that you’ll eat pretty much anything, but that you love a good pizza. So, I thought we’d make our own today. How does that sound?” She asks him, laying out all the toppings Shane had told her Rozanov would want. 

Rozanov nods, a small smile making its way onto his face. “Will Shane have one too?” He asks, looking like he already has an answer. Yuna doesn’t comment on how familiar he seems with her son’s mannerisms. 

“He’s having some dish that David found that fits his diet,” she says, sectioning out the dough into three different chunks. “You know how he is.” Because it has become increasingly clear that Rozanov does know how Shane is. 

“Yes, I know,” Rozanov smiles. “I tell him all the time, one pizza will not kill you.” He pauses, shrugging innocently. “Then he gives me a look and I do not argue anymore.” 

Yuna can’t help the laugh that bursts out of her. “That’s very wise of you, Rozanov.” 

“Ilya,” he corrects, causing her to look up from where she’s spreading flour on the counter. The smile is gone from his face, but he doesn’t look angry. Yuna thinks a better name for it is sincere. 

“It’s nice to properly meet you, Ilya. I’m Yuna,” she smiles warmly. 

Ilya doesn’t say anything else, but he returns her smile in earnest. He looks a lot younger than he does when he’s on the ice, and she feels a familiar need to protect him washing over her. It’s how she felt the first time Shane came back home after signing with the Voyageurs, and she found him curled up and asleep in his childhood bed. He looked so small, and so young, and she had never felt more terrified of sending him back out into the real world, where anyone could say or do anything, and she wouldn’t be there to shield him from it anymore. 

The fact that she’s feeling that same maternal wave of protective instincts course through her while watching Ilya roll pizza dough out on her kitchen counter is something she decides to put aside for later. For now, she focuses on assembling her own pizza. 

Sometime after they begin to top their pizzas, David returns from work, and joins them in the kitchen. 

“What have we got here?” He asks, placing a quick peck on Yuna’s cheek as he moves to wash his hands in the sink. “Are we making pizzas?” 

“We are,” she responds, reaching for the pepperoni. “Shane mentioned that pizza is one of Ilya’s favorite foods, so I figured that this could be fun.” 

“I’ll never say no to pizza,” David says with a grin, beginning to roll out his dough. “You and I have that in common, it sounds like,” he nods at Ilya, who gives a small smile in return. 

They make small talk while working on their pizzas, David giving a short recap of his day, and complimenting Ilya’s choice of flowers. Ilya blushes and does his best to give Shane credit for them, but David just waves him off, and tells him that he’s grateful anyways. Yuna and Ilya get into a passionate debate about whether or not fruit belongs on pizza, which gets heated enough to distract all three of them from the sound of the front door opening and closing. Right as Ilya is about to make his counterargument, Shane appears in the doorway, his eyes wide with shock.  

“Holy shit, you guys are like, actually getting along,” he says incredulously. It would be endearing, if it didn’t also imply that he had assumed either Ilya or his parents were incapable of getting along with one another. 

“Do you really think that lowly of us, Shane?” David jokes, “we’re all adults here, aren’t we?”

“Plus, Ilya’s proven to be very good company,” Yuna defends, not missing the proud look that flashes across Ilya’s face when she does so. 

“He has?” Shane asks, his voice cracking the way it usually does when he can’t believe what he’s saying. He turns to Ilya, a softer look on his face, “you have?” This time, though, his voice is gentle, as if he’s approaching a small animal.

Ilya just shrugs, a smile forcing its way onto his face. Shane’s eyes are doing the thing where he tries very hard not to cry, and does a very bad job at hiding it. Yuna just wants to know what the fuck is going on between the two of them. 

“He brought flowers, too,” she tacks on, popping whatever bubble the two of them had escaped to for a moment. Shane’s gaze falls on the vase of tulips, and his smile grows ten times wider. 

“He did,” he says, more to himself than anyone else. Turning to face his parents, he looks genuinely apologetic when he says, “please turn around.” Then, before Yuna can ask what he’s talking about, Shane is launching himself at Ilya, and yanking him down into a kiss with a hand fisted in his shirt.  

Yuna doesn’t mean to stare, but she’s more than a bit in shock. A quick glance at David tells her that he’s just as surprised as she is to see their son so apparently comfortable with such a public display of affection. It’s chaste, sure, but it’s still passionate, and Ilya melts into it like it’s second nature. The image of them so wrapped up in each other is just another piece of evidence that this thing between them— whatever it may be— isn’t new. In fact, Yuna’s starting to suspect that it’s much older than she had originally thought. 

When they finally pull back, both their faces are red, and Shane is pointedly keeping his eyes trained on Ilya. “They didn’t turn around, did they?” He asks, and Ilya does a terrible job of hiding the smug look on his face. 

“You are correct, moya lyubov,” he says with a smirk, shooting Yuna and David a look. “They did not.” 

Shane swats him in the arm, muttering something in Russian, which causes Ilya to let out a loud laugh. Yuna makes a mental note to get started on learning Russian. Because she’s nosy, but also because she wants Ilya to feel like he can freely express himself  around them, without having to think twice about how to word it in a way they will understand. Mostly because she’s nosy, though. 

“That answers like, at least five of the questions I had,” David says, breaking the tension that’s settled over the room. 

“Right, questions,” Shane says, finally turning to face them. “You probably have a lot of those.” 

His face is still redder than usual, but it doesn’t reach the tips of his ears anymore, which is a good sign that he’s not as panicked as Yuna would typically expect him to be about something like this. Not that there’s any precedent for their current situation, but still. For someone whose resting state is inherently anxious, Shane seems unusually calm. She assumes it has probably has something to do with Ilya, by the way Shane seems to relax into his touch, and she can’t help but marvel at the fact that her son, who has spent nearly his whole life averted to touch, has found someone whose touch makes him melt, rather than flinch. 

“Just a few,” Yuna says, “why don’t we head to the dining room? We can talk there?” Shane nods, and Ilya seems happy to follow his lead when he shuffles out of the kitchen. David shoots her a look, and gives her shoulder a firm squeeze— a silent promise that whatever it is they’re about to walk into, they walk into together. 

When they enter the dining room, Yuna can’t help but notice that Shane’s leg is bouncing up and down. She watches as Ilya takes notice as well, before his arm is moving under the table, and suddenly, Shane’s leg comes to a halt. Presumably because of Ilya’s touch. Something that Yuna still doesn’t know how to digest. Yuna and David take their seats opposite to Shane and Ilya, and silence settles over the room again. 

Yuna takes in the scene before her. Ilya’s looking at Shane like he’ll follow his lead, regardless of where it goes. It makes her stomach hurt, thinking about how long she’s waited for Shane to like someone enough to bring them home. His archrival wasn’t exactly her first prediction, but she doesn’t really give a fuck who he brings home. She just wants him to be loved, and it’s become clear that he is. 

“So, how long have you two been together?” She decides is a decent question to start with. 

Ilya and Shane exchange a look, and Ilya shrugs, as if Shane’s the only one who knows the answer. Shane’s face looks mildly irritated that Ilya’s making him take the wheel on answering, and Yuna can’t help but find it all a bit amusing. By the way they’re acting, she wouldn’t be surprised if Shane told her they’d been married for at least three years now. 

“Like, officially together?” Shane asks, a cautious look on his face. 

Yuna raises a brow in confusion. “I mean… yes?” She’s starting to realize that the history between the two of them may be much more complicated than she originally thought. 

“Almost a year now,” Shane replies, then. 

Which can’t be right, Yuna thinks. They’re way too comfortable with each other for that to be true. So, because she is nothing if not nosy, she pries. 

“Were you not official before that?” 

Shane squirms in his seat, his hands clearly wringing together where they sit in his lap. “Well,” he starts, but Ilya reaches across and stops his hands from moving. 

“We did not label it for a long time,” he says, his eyes flicking between Yuna and Shane. “But I have been in love with him the whole time.” 

Yuna feels sick. “And how long is ‘the whole time?’”

It’s silent for a moment, before Shane speaks up. “After we filmed that commercial together. Right before our rookie season.” 

Yuna looks at them— poorly concealed nerves and hands clasped under the table— and realizes that ‘right before their rookie season’ was nearly a decade ago. Jesus Christ. 

“No, before that,” Ilya interjects, looking offended by Shane’s apparent mistake in their relationship’s timeline. Shane looks just as lost as Yuna is. 

“They’re not asking about when we met, Ilya,” he says, “they mean, like, the first time we—” He pauses, sighs, and brings a hand up to pinch the bridge of his nose. Gesturing to the air between him and Ilya, he tries to silently communicate the word sex without saying it out loud. Yuna finds it quite entertaining. One glance at Ilya tells her that he agrees with her amusement. 

“First time we what, solnyshko?” Ilya goads, smirking at the irritated look Shane shoots at him. 

“You are so aggravating,” Shane groans, letting out the deepest sigh Yuna has ever heard, and burying his head in his hands. Ilya smiles brighter than the sun, and David chokes on a laugh next to her. 

“That means ‘I love you’ in Shane language,” Ilya says smugly, leaning back in his chair and crossing his arms over his chest. They’re such a ridiculous pair, and yet, they work terrifyingly well together. In fact, the longer Yuna observes them, the more they make sense. 

Ilya clearly knows Shane’s limits, and which ones he’s allowed to test, before it goes too far. He is one of the only people who can understand Shane’s work life, as well as his personal life, and he seems to have a better handle on balancing the two, than Shane himself. His touch alone can bring Shane back from a ledge, and he has a charming sense of humor. He teases Shane, just as much as he calms him and takes care of him. Really, it’s no wonder Shane wound up falling for him. Yuna wouldn’t doubt him if he told her it felt inevitable. 

“I do not have a language, asshole,” Shane snaps, shooting Ilya a sideways glare. 

“Yes you do,” Ilya insists, “you say ‘Ilya, you irritate me,’ and it means you adore me. You say ‘Ilya, do not even think about it,’ and it means ‘do it.’ Is language of your people, Shane. You should know it better than me.” 

Yuna cannot comprehend whatever the hell she’s watching go down between them. All she knows is that Shane does have a tendency to say things that don’t reveal how he really feels, in order to preserve his emotions, and the fact that Ilya has that tendency noted, understood, and damn near memorized, is genuinely impressive. To be loved is to be known, she thinks, not for the first time today. 

“Wait,” she says abruptly, a memory flashing through her mind. “You said the first time was after you filmed that commercial?” 

Shane gives her a confused look, and Ilya mirrors it, until he doesn’t, and his expression fades into understanding. 

“I think I know what you are going to ask,” he says, a small smile playing on his lips. 

“When I ran into you in the elevator,” she continues, her brain putting all the pieces together. “Was that…were you?” 

Shane looks horrified, while Ilya tries very hard to conceal a laugh. 

“What do you mean, when you ran into him in the elevator?” Shane asks, his voice cracking. Ilya has his head tilted back so that he’s looking straight at the ceiling, his cheeks puffed out as he holds his breath to keep from bursting out laughing. He looks exactly like he did when Yuna saw him in that elevator, which she now has confirmation was the first time they got together. 

“You were going up…” Yuna trails off, because it’s clear that her assumptions are correct. 

Shane reaches out, takes Ilya’s chin in his hand, and pulls his head down so that Shane can look him, directly in his eyes. “Ilya. What is she talking about.” He states, like it isn’t a question that Ilya’s allowed to leave unanswered, and Yuna’s just glad that it seems like Ilya’s enjoying it. 

“Is nothing, lyubimyy,” he says, which does not let him off the hook, whatsoever. When his face is still held firmly in place by a very irritated Shane, he rolls his eyes, and pouts. “I will tell you later, yes? I do not think you will want to talk about it in front of your parents.”

Ilya’s still hamming up the dramatics, but his voice is soft, like he wants Shane to understand why he’s dismissing the topic, as of right now. It’s sweet, and a caution that most people don’t think to take when communicating with Shane, because most people don’t understand that he needs things spelled out for him. Ilya, apparently, is not like most people. Shane doesn’t do well with reading between lines, or catching undertones, and Ilya seems to understand this, if the way he accommodates his voice and his tone Shane’s mental processes is anything to go by. 

Something in Yuna settles at the realization that Ilya has the patience to stick around long enough for Shane to communicate his needs with him. She knows her son very well, and he does not open up to people easily. He has a hard time asking for what he wants, and describing the things that he needs from other people. It takes a while for Shane to be comfortable with people, and she’s relieved by the fact that Ilya has stayed long enough to become one of those people. 

Shane seems to accept Ilya’s explanation, letting him lean in to press a quick kiss to Shane’s mouth before they both turn to face Yuna and David again. 

—

When they’ve finished eating lunch, the moving truck full of Ilya’s belongings pulls up outside the house next door, and what she’s about to do finally hits Yuna. 

She’s moving her son’s boyfriend into the house next door to hers, because he made the wild decision to sign with a worse team in order to be closer to her son. It’s sweet, from the perspective of a parent. However, from the perspective of a lifelong hockey fan, it’s an incredibly stupid move. Which is what makes Ilya’s confidence in his decision so much more heartwarming, she supposes.  

Ilya has a lot of stuff, it turns out. 

David and Ilya have taken furniture duty, while Shane and Yuna are left to deal with the boxes. She figures it’s as good a time to talk as she’s going to get, but she doesn’t exactly know what to start with. 

Fortunately for her, Shane asks the first question. 

“Are you really okay with this? With me and Ilya?” He asks, tentatively. She hates how scared he appears to be. She hates that he’s asking if she’s okay with him being in love. Everything his question implies feels wrong, but she supposes that’s her fault, isn’t it? 

“Should I not be?” She replies, reaching for the other side of the box he’s holding, offering to help carry it inside. 

“I mean,” Shane winces, leading her to lower the box onto the floor of what Yuna assumes will become a living room. “It’s Ilya.”  He’s refusing to make eye contact with her, and for a moment, the worst flashes through her mind. 

“Shane, does he hurt you?” 

Shane’s head snaps up, and his eyes go wide. “No!” He exclaims, clearing his throat. “No, of course not, he would never do that. He’s—” 

Shane cuts himself off, and his cheeks flush. Yuna reaches across the carpet, and gently takes his hand into her own. The move causes Shane to meet her eyes, a small smile beginning to grow. 

“He’s so good to me, mom,” he says softly, as if he’s testing the words out for the first time. It occurs to Yuna that it might genuinely be the first time he’s saying them, and she tries not to spook him by pressing for more. In the past 48 hours, Shane has experienced more changes than he would usually be able to handle before having a meltdown. Yuna has an inkling that the man currently helping her husband carry a headboard into his new house has something to do with how calm Shane’s managed to stay throughout all of it. 

“I know the media makes him seem like this aggressive, violent person with anger issues and a bad temper, but that’s— he’s the complete opposite.” Shane swallows hard, his eyes shining with unshed tears. 

“He’s gentle, and kind, and he never yells, even when I give him plenty of reasons to. And he’s so patient with me, especially when I get overwhelmed, and he’s the only person who’s ever cared to ask what I need in those moments. Life hasn’t exactly been kind to him, but still, he’s good.

“And I know they always say we hate each other, but we don’t. I never did, really. I love him more than anyone on this earth, and the only thing I truly hate is how most people refuse to see how good he is.” 

Yuna can’t imagine how she would feel if the media made Shane out to be even half as bad as they make Ilya out to be. She thinks about how sick it would make her feel if anyone were to suggest that David was some kind of monster, and, to her horror, she realizes that she’s played into the version of Ilya that the media has convinced the public is genuine.           

“Shane,” she starts, swallowing the lump in her throat that she didn’t realize was there. “I am so, so glad that you’ve found someone who loves you the way he so clearly does.” She pauses, her voice thick with the tears that she’s trying so hard not to let fall. “And I am sorry that I made you feel like you couldn’t tell me about him until now.” 

She meets his gaze, watching as a tear slips down his cheek. His lips are pressed into a quivering smile, and he makes no move to wipe his face as more escape. 

“I was going to tell you, I promise,” Shane says, his voice barely above a whisper. “I was just so scared that you would…” he trails off, as if what he was going to say tasted too bitter in his mouth. He swallows hard, and tries again. “I wanted you to see him as the Ilya I love. Not the Rozanov you hate.”  

Her breath catches in her throat. “Shane,” she sighs, reaching her free hand out to cradle his face. Her thumb catches a stray tear, and she gently wipes it away. “I could never hate anyone that you love. I wish I had done more to make you believe that, but I will say it now, as many times as you need me to, until it sticks.”    

Shane takes a deep breath, and closes his eyes. When he was little, he used to get so upset, but he would try so hard to hide it. He would close his eyes, and take the deepest breaths a six year old was capable of, using his fingers to silently count how long to inhale, hold, and exhale for. As he got older, Yuna learned that the trick to getting him to open up was patience. Shane would sit somewhere quiet, counting his breathing with his fingers, and keeping his eyes shut, as if the world was too much to deal with. And Yuna would wait for however long it took for him to come to her on his own, and he would lay his head on her lap, and finally let her in. So, when Shane finally folds, and curls into her embrace, it feels inevitable.     

His head is resting on her chest, just under her chin, and her arms are wrapped securely around his shoulders. Neither of them say anything as she rubs his back in the same soothing way that used to put him to sleep when he was a kid. The sound of David and Ilya situating furniture upstairs is welcome background noise, and when a particularly loud thump sounds through the empty house, an even louder laugh follows it. 

“We’re okay!” Ilya yells, and Shane begins to laugh softly against her collarbone. 

“Ilya dropped a mirror,” David shouts, causing her to smile into Shane’s hair. 

“That is a lie, he is a liar who is lying to you,” Ilya immediately protests, the entire argument occurring out of sight. It effectively puts Shane in a fit of giggles, and Yuna silently thanks them for the change in mood.  

“Does he always do that?” She says, turning her head just enough to be able to look down at Shane. She didn’t even know he was capable of smiling as wide as he is right now, and the lovesick look that takes over her son’s face makes her heart ache. 

Shane just shrugs. “Why do you think I fell in love with him?”

The words fall out of his mouth like they’ve been waiting to do so for a while now, and Yuna hopes that casual declarations of love will never stop spilling out of her son.    

—

A week later, Yuna opens the fridge, only to find that they’re out of milk. She considers running to the store, but it’s early, and she’d rather not drive all the way there, only to buy one thing. Her second option, thankfully, just moved in next door. 

She expects Shane to answer the door, since it’s still quite early, and Ilya does not strike her as the type to wake up during the off season just to go on a run. That saying about opposites attracting is eerily applicable to their relationship, she’s discovered. Where Shane is a morning person, Ilya would rather laze in bed until his stomach demands he get up and eat. 

So, when Ilya opens the door, she’s a bit surprised. It is technically his house, but it’s also 7:15 in the morning. She knows that Shane has been staying over since the move, and she can still see his car in the driveway, so Ilya being the one to answer the door is unexpected, but not unwelcome. 

“Yuna,” he greets her, a smile breaking out across his face. He’s shirtless, wearing a pair of worn sweatpants with Boston’s logo on the side, and Yuna can’t help but notice the giant bear he has tattooed across his pec. “I was not expecting you,” Ilya says sheepishly. There’s a mark just below his collarbone that looks suspiciously like a hickey, but she knows better than to comment on it.    

“I could say the same for you,” she smiles, “isn’t it a bit early for you?” 

Ilya shrugs, and gestures for her to step inside. “Still adjusting to the new house, I think,” he says, watching as she slips her shoes off. “If you need Shane, I can go wake him up.” 

“He’s still sleeping?” She replies, shocked to hear that her son is apparently breaking the rigid routine he’s had since he got drafted all those years ago.

“He sleeps in an hour later during off season,” Ilya explains, as if this is something that everyone knows about Shane. Everyone but Yuna, apparently. Ilya’s cheeks go a little red, and he scratches nervously at the back of his neck. “I told him the bed is cold when he is not there.” 

Which is the stupidest, sweetest thing Yuna has ever heard. 

Her Shane, who has adhered to some kind of routine his whole life, and who gets anxious at the slightest hint of change, has found someone who makes change less scary. It hits her, suddenly, that Shane isn’t just hers anymore. Because her Shane hated change, but Ilya’s Shane agreed to wake up an entire hour later than usual, just so he could spend more time in bed with his boyfriend. 

She can’t help but smile at Ilya’s admission. “That’s alright, then. I just came to ask if you two had any milk I could borrow. We ran out, and it’s a little early to run to the store.” 

“Of course,” Ilya nods, gesturing for her to follow him into the kitchen. 

Upon entering, it becomes clear that there’s still a long way to go on unpacking. There are boxes piled near the pantry door, and the glass cabinets showcase barely any dishware. 

“Do you still need help unpacking?” Yuna asks, looking around the nearly-empty kitchen. Ilya follows her gaze, his face flushing a bit.       

“You do not have to help. I will do it eventually,” he says, looking a bit ashamed at the fact that she noticed. 

“I don’t have to, but I’m offering,” she pushes, sensing there was something underlying Ilya’s denial. “It’s okay to ask for help, Ilya,” she assures him. The way he looks at her in return tells her that he doesn’t hear that often. 

“Shane tells me this too,” he eventually responds, forcing a smile. She can tell he’s trying to make light of a situation that could quickly go downhill, but it doesn’t make her heart hurt for him any less. “I am working on believing it,” Ilya promises. Yuna trusts him. 

“He learned from the best,” she says with a wink. It makes Ilya smile, so she counts it as a win, and makes a mental note to ask Shane about it later. “How about you make some coffee, and I start unpacking this first box?” She suggests, already reaching out to open the box sitting on the top of the stack. 

Ilya nods. “Deal.”  

They work in silence, Yuna sorting silverware, and Ilya getting the coffeemaker up and running. He joins her eventually, opening the next box in the stack, and beginning to unload an assortment of mugs into the cabinet above them. It’s nice, Yuna thinks, to be able to exist like this. She feels no pressure to start a conversation, and Ilya doesn’t force one either. When she’s finished with the silverware, she joins him in unpacking dishes. 

“This past week,” Ilya breaks the silence, continuing to unpack. “I was not…okay. Is why I did not get to unpack yet.” 

Yuna pauses, setting down the mug that she’d just picked out of the box. “Not okay?” She asks softly, giving him every indication that he’s safe to continue. Being vulnerable with her son’s boyfriend that she only just found out about a week ago wasn’t exactly on schedule, but she was more than willing to make room for it.  

Ilya’s stopped unpacking now, his face lost in thought. It hits her that he’s probably trying to figure out how to articulate what he’s feeling in English. She gives him all the time he needs, because Ilya is now her son by extension, and if she can be patient with Shane, she can be patient with him. 

“I have depression,” Ilya admits. “Some days are worse than others. And when I am really sad, it feels like I can’t do anything else. It’s—” he cuts himself off, his eyes cast downwards. “I can’t control it.”

Yuna takes him in. He looks so different from what everyone else seems to see. Sitting cross legged on his kitchen floor, a light blue mug with a poorly painted ‘S’ on it clutched tightly in his hands; he looks so young. And yes, she knows he’s the same age as Shane, but still. Before she is anything else, she is a mother. And that’s what Ilya seems to need right now.  

“Thank you for trusting me with that part of you, Ilya,” she says, reaching a hand out towards him and leaving it face up, giving him the opportunity to accept the help she’s offering. He takes it, and his hand makes hers seem much smaller than it really is when he tentatively takes it. “There is nothing wrong with having depression, or needing a little extra help from time to time.” 

“I feel broken, sometimes.” 

Yuna’s heart clenches. “I know, hon.” 

Ilya lifts his gaze, the sadness so extremely pronounced in them that it feels like she’s looking at a completely different person. “Do you know how to make it stop feeling so bad?” 

Her breath catches in her throat, and she feels the question down to her core. “I don’t know if there’s any way to ever completely ‘stop’ it,” she tells him honestly. She feels her heart sink, weighing heavy on her chest. One admission from him, and she’s nineteen again, staring up at the ceiling of her dorm room, hoping that sometime soon, she’ll find the motivation to keep going. She sees herself in him, and looking at him feels a bit like looking into a mirror warped by time.   

“I don’t want to be like this forever,” Ilya says, his voice so soft that Yuna nearly misses it. 

“I know,” she replies, using her free hand to move the mug out of his hands, and place it back in the box. She uses their conjoined hands to guide him into leaning back against the kitchen island, settling in shoulder to shoulder with him. She lets go of his hand, and wraps her arm around his frame, pulling him in until he rests his head on her shoulder. It’s silent between them for a moment, and Yuna doesn’t comment on how Ilya’s exhales have gotten shaky.

“You know what, Ilya?” She starts, bringing a hand up to scratch at his scalp. It always used to calm Shane down when he was worked up. She figures Ilya won’t be too different. “I was a lot like you when I was younger.” 

She feels Ilya’s breath stutter. “Really?” 

“Really,” she confirms. “I used to skate too. Not hockey— I figure skated. I was good, too.” 

“So you think I’m good,” Ilya quips, and she can practically hear the smirk in his voice. 

“Don’t put words in my mouth,” she jokes, pinching his side, and causing him to squirm. “I was, though. A good skater. And I wanted to be the best.” 

“There is a but,” Ilya says quietly. Yuna is starting to catch on to his unique ability to read people.

“Is there?”

“There always is.” Ilya goes silent, his hands fidgeting with the hem of his shirt. “I am good at hockey, but I am so sad all the time. You were good at skating, but…” he trails off, compelling her to fill in the blank. 

She nods, rubbing a hand up and down his arm. “I was good at skating. But I was very sad, a lot of the time.” 

Ilya tenses underneath her touch, and she squeezes his shoulder in comfort. 

“You?” Ilya asks, his head turning to look up at her. He looks so young, and her heart aches. 

“Me,” she confirms with a sad nod. “I was diagnosed when I was seventeen.”  

“I met Shane when I was seventeen,” Ilya admits, softly. 

Yuna feels a lump form in her throat. She didn’t know they’d been in each other’s orbits that long. She can only imagine how terrifying it must have been, to be so young, and to be face to face with someone that you want so badly, while simultaneously feeling a profound sense of sadness. She remembers the first time she saw David, and the sadness in her felt even heavier than usual. Like a reminder that no matter who she loved, and no matter how much she loved them, it would always be there; love could not fix it. Love could not fix her. 

“The world is a lot scarier, at seventeen,” she nods. “If something bad happens to you when you’re young, it feels like the world is ending.” 

Ilya nods slowly, his head still resting on her shoulder. His curls tickle her chin, but she makes no move to get up. His weight against her side is comforting, and she has a feeling her presence is soothing him as well. 

“But you’re not seventeen anymore, Ilya.” She pauses, combing through his hair with a gentle hand. “You’re not seventeen, and the world didn’t end, and you are loved. By more people than I think you realize.” 

It’s then, when she feels something wet drop onto her collarbone. She feels his body shake a bit with the shuddering breath that he takes, and she realizes it then— Ilya is crying. She relishes in the fact that he’s deemed her a safe person to cry with. Lord knows she’d needed one of those when she was in his spot. 

“Yes, you are sad. That’s how depression works; you are sad a lot of the time, and you can’t control it. And it’s debilitating, and exhausting, and it will tell you that there’s no point in going on.” 

Ilya inhales sharply. “Debilitating?” 

“It makes you feel like you’re weak. Like you can’t move, or do anything,” she explains. 

“Ah, yes,” Ilya nods, then. “Debilitating. Depression is very much that.” She smiles, and he relaxes against her again. 

“It is. And it doesn’t ever really stop being like that, unfortunately,” she tells him, fighting back her own tears. 

“You are still sad like me?” Ilya’s voice sounds small. It hurts more than she’d like to admit. 

“The bad days don’t happen as often anymore, but I still carry it with me, everywhere I go. It’s impossible not to. There are still days where it hits me harder than usual, and I feel like I can barely move. Even now.” 

“So,” Ilya starts, sounding dejected. “This sadness. It is forever?” 

Yuna shakes her head, knowing he can feel it. “It will probably live within you forever, yes. I’m guessing the thing that makes you the most sad is not something that will ever be undone?” Ilya nods, confirming what she’d already assumed. She won’t pry this time, because she hopes it will come out in time. Most things do, after all. 

“The sadness will always be there, yes. But it does not have to control you. The world doesn’t have to end if you don’t want it to.” 

“I don’t want it to. But I am scared that is not enough.” 

“It is. You are enough,” she says firmly, hoping that he’ll believe it someday. “And, on the days that it feels a little heavier, you are allowed to ask for help carrying it.” 

Ilya’s eyes are trained on the tiles of the kitchen floor, refusing to meet hers. “I do not want to burden anyone.” 

“I know. But the people who love you don’t see taking care of you as a burden. Because they love you, Ilya.”  

Ilya doesn’t respond, but he leans into her when her hand comes up to stroke his hair again, so she assumes she didn’t startle him too much. 

“You are very loved, you know that?” She knows he doesn’t. She asks anyway. 

“I am not sure I deserve it,” he mumbles, devastatingly. “And I am terrified that one day, he will realize I am not worth it. That my sadness is too much.” 

“I think he’d much rather you be sad, than dead.” 

The statement sits heavily in the air, far too true for Ilya to even try and dispute it. 

“You know,” Yuna breaks the silence between them, turning her head to press a soft kiss to his hairline. “I can tell how much he really loves you.” 

“How?” Ilya asks, his voice timid. 

“Because it’s eight AM on a Friday, and he’s still asleep.” 

—

Shane finds them in a fully unpacked kitchen, halfway through the process of making breakfast. Ilya’s got a spatula in one hand, and a ladle in the other, and he’s standing at the stove watching batter become a pancake in a pan. Yuna is hovering beside him, dropping a handful of chocolate chips into the batter, and telling him to stop trying to flip it too early, because it won’t make you look cool, it’ll only ruin our breakfast.      

“Mom?” Shane says, his voice raspy. He flushes, and immediately clears his throat. Yuna does her best  not to think of the implications. 

“Hey honey,” she greets him with a smile. “We’re out of milk, so I came to ask if you had some we could borrow.”  

Shane nods. “It should be on the third shelf of the fridge door,” he states confidently, as if he already knows the house by heart. 

As if it were his own, her brain unhelpfully supplies. She knows that right now, it’s only Ilya who’s moving in, but she can’t help from wondering what it would be like to have both of them playing in Ottawa. One day, maybe. For now, one of them was enough. Baby steps. 

“I’ll grab it on my way out,” Yuna assures him, patting Ilya’s arm and signaling him to flip the pancake. Shane stares at the two of them from the doorway of the kitchen, as if the image of them working together isn’t computing in his head. “I noticed how many boxes Ilya still had left to unpack. I thought I’d help make it feel like a little less work,” she explains. 

Shane just stares at them, his mouth parted slightly, as if he wants to say something, but can’t. “Why didn’t you wake me?” He settles for, his question directed at Ilya. 

Ilya flips the pancake, and shrugs. “You have not slept much lately. You looked too peaceful,” Ilya replies with an innocent shrug. 

Shane’s expression softens, and his mouth twitches with a small smile. Yuna watches as they silently communicate across the kitchen, feeling incredibly left out of something she doesn’t even want to be included in. 

“I wouldn’t have minded,” Shane protests. 

“Was just your mother asking to borrow milk,” Ilya rolls his eyes. “When I open the door and a robber holds me at gunpoint, I will come get you.” 

Shane’s face breaks out into a wide smile. “Asshole.” 

“Love you too,” Ilya smirks, allowing Yuna to take the spatula from him. She plops the pancake on top of the already accumulating stack, and watches as Shane makes his way over to where they’re standing. 

He stops in front of Ilya, and, without sparing Yuna a second glance, reaches up to pull him in for a kiss. Ilya immediately melts into his embrace, and Yuna can’t help but smile. Knowing what she knows about Ilya, and knowing what she knows about Shane, she doesn’t see any reason to be anything but happy that they’ve found each other. When the two of them pull back, Shane keeps his arms wrapped around Ilya’s shoulders, their foreheads brushing.   

“Your daily cup of poison and throw up is in the fridge,” Ilya says, earning him a swat to the back of his head. 

“Would you still kiss me even if I actually drank poison and throw up for breakfast everyday?” Shane asks. Yuna’s pretty sure everyone in the room knows the answer. Ilya pathetically supplies him with it anyway. 

“Yes. Just like how you would still love me if I was a worm.” 

“What?” Yuna cuts in, unable to let that one go without a bit of context. 

Shane sighs, dropping his arms from where they’ve been wrapped around Ilya’s neck. “I don’t know. He asks me random things like that all the time. I’ve learned to just go with it.”  

“Is important that I know the extent of his dedication to me,” Ilya shrugs, turning off the stove, and moving to grab three plates out of the cabinet. 

“Whatever helps you sleep at night,” Shane says passively, like they’ve had this conversation a million times over. 

“A warm bath and good sex,” Ilya says, a smug look on his face. 

Shane closes his eyes, and takes a deep breath. “Ilya.” 

“Yes, sweetheart?” 

“Go set the fucking table.”

—

On the day of Ottawa’s home opener, Yuna tells Ilya that she and David will be cheering him on from their living room.

Then, she gets in the car, searches the Tire Centre on Google maps, and presses Go on the fastest route. 

The arena is packed— much fuller than any game Yuna’s ever been to in her entire career as a Centaurs fan. Which was mostly due to the fact that, for a while, the Ottawa Centaurs have sat comfortably at the bottom of the league’s rankings. However, she has faith that after this season, that will no longer be the case.    

For all the hatred that Yuna holds for the Boston Bears, though, she would be lying if she said that Ilya Rozanov was a bad team captain. After all, Boston hadn’t won a cup in years when they drafted him, and by the time he left, they had one more Stanley Cup under their belt, and they’d made it to the playoffs every other year since he’d signed. 

Ilya had a universal charm about him, and Yuna is yet to meet anyone immune to it. The consensus seems to be that if you know him, you love him. He has a unique ability to not only read people, but to truly see them. You don’t win Stanley Cups by being an asshole to your players and abusing your power. Ilya knows that more than anyone, and it shows in his teams’ performance. 

Sitting high up in the stands, she hears the announcer call Ilya’s name over the loudspeaker. Without hesitation, her and David are out of their seats, cheering so loud that he might as well be able to hear them from the ice. A familiar feeling blooms in her chest, and it feels nice to have another person to be proud of. 

Ottawa loses, which isn’t unexpected, but Ilya scores a hat trick, and plays some damn good hockey overall. 

They wait outside in the parking lot, hanging back as discreetly as they can. Ilya’s one of the last one’s out of the arena, his head bent towards the ground, bag slung over his shoulder. 

“Ilya!” David pipes up, calling out to him across the parking lot. 

Ilya’s head jerks in their direction, and his eyes light up. “You came?” 

“We weren’t sure if you’d be okay with it,” Yuna says, taking the wide smile on his face as a sign that Ilya is perfectly okay with it. 

When he reaches them, they’re swept into an enormous hug. Yuna can’t remember the last time she was hugged this tightly. It feels nice. 

“No one has come to watch me in a very long time,” Ilya says softly, his voice muffled from where he’s buried his face in her neck. All Yuna can do is hug him even tighter. 

They pull back, and she feels herself holding back tears.

“We’d love to come to as many as we can make it to,” David tells Ilya, squeezing him on the shoulder that isn’t occupied by his bag. “It was nice to not have to drive two hours to cheer our son on, this time.” Yuna watches as the subtle slip dawns on Ilya, and prays that they aren’t overstepping. By the way Ilya pulls them both into another bone-crushing hug, she doesn’t think they are. 

—

“Ilya told me you went to his game last night,” Shane tells her over the phone the next day. “You didn’t have to do that, but he wants you to know he appreciates it. A lot.” 

“I know we didn’t have to, Shane,” she assures him. The last thing she wants is for either of them to think that what Yuna and David do to make Ilya feel welcome in their family is all rooted in pity. “We wanted to, though.” 

There’s a pause on Shane’s end. “I haven’t told you about his home life, because it’s not my thing to tell, but I think it’s pretty public knowledge that his parents are both dead. So you and dad—” Shane stutters, his breath catching, “what you did yesterday meant a lot. People haven’t really shown up for him like that in a long time.”   

“I’m glad we didn’t overstep,” she smiles. 

“Not at all,” Shane assures her, “I think he’s just grateful you even made it to one.” 

The comment makes her think about what David had told Ilya earlier. We’d love to come to as many as we can make it to. 

She feels a lump form in her throat. Swallowing it down, she reaches for her laptop, and renews the season tickets she and David had bought back when they first moved to Ottawa. They’d cancelled them after Shane got drafted to Montreal, because the Centaurs had sucked for years, and their son would soon be their opponent— they had no reason to attend games as often as they used to, anymore. 

When the confirmation email comes in, she can’t help but feel excited that they have a reason again.  

—

Shane calls her after he and Ilya have their first real fight, and asks her to check on Ilya. His voice shakes, and she can tell that he’s crying, and before she can even ask, he’s telling her that it’s his fault, and that he doesn’t want Ilya to be alone right now. 

He still doesn’t know that Ilya told her about his depression all those weeks ago on the kitchen floor, and she doesn’t plan on being the one to tell him. It’s Ilya’s thing to tell, she figures. 

She doesn’t have to go far on her search for Ilya. She finds him sitting on the steps of his front porch, a cigarette in one hand, the other fidgeting with the pack it came from. His head is tilted downward, and when he looks up to take a drag, his eyes meet hers. 

“Please do not tell Shane,” he says, sounding as exhausted as he looks. His eyes are red-rimmed, like he’s been crying too, but she can’t help from smiling at his request. 

“Wasn’t going to,” she says, sitting down beside him. She puts her hand out, gesturing for him to hand her a cigarette, and he stares at her in disbelief when he puts together what she’s asking him to do. “I thought we’d been over this; I was young and depressed once, too,” she jokes, nudging him gently with an elbow. 

Ilya stares at her, seemingly analyzing her face, as if he’s double checking that she’s not trying to catch him in a gotcha moment. When he decides that she’s being genuine, his face breaks out into a wide smile, and he lets out a soft laugh. 

“You are much cooler than your son, you know,” he tells her, handing her a cigarette and a lighter. 

She lights it, takes a drag, and exhales. It’s been a while since she indulged the part of her that used to need this so badly, and it feels nostalgic in a bittersweet way. She’s not who she used to be— she’s not who she used to be.   

“Between me and you,” she says, turning her head to look over at him, “that’s a pretty low bar to clear.” Ilya throws his head back in a hearty laugh, and she counts it as a win. 

“You are funnier than you look, Mrs. Hollander.” 

“Yuna,” she corrects him, giving him the same look she has every other time he’s called her Mrs. Hollander. He blushes every time she scolds him about it, but she knows he secretly needs the reminder that yes, you’re allowed to see me as family. 

“Yuna,” Ilya echoes, nodding his head obediently. “Shane sent you here, didn’t he?”

Yuna shrugs. “Are you surprised?”   

“No,” Ilya sighs, blowing out a puff of smoke as he does. “He worries about me.” 

“To be fair,” she holds back a smile, “Shane worries about everything.”

Ilya smirks. “Are you saying I’m not special?” 

“No,” she shakes her head. “I’m saying that Shane worrying is a given, regardless of what the situation is. Worrying is his love language.” 

Ilya snorts, staring straight ahead. He hasn’t met her gaze once this entire time, and she has a feeling it has something to do with shame. Shame that is unfounded, but not surprising, based on the few things she’s heard about Ilya’s upbringing. 

“He doesn’t want me to smoke anymore,” Ilya tells her, his face void of any readable emotion. 

“He doesn’t want you to die,” she corrects him, because she knows Shane, and his tendency to catastrophize. 

Ilya doesn’t respond, the words hanging in the air, heavy and haunting. His eyes narrow on something in the distance. He taps the ash out of his cigarette, and puts it out on the pavement more aggressively than necessary. She can tell when he realizes how rough he’s being with an inanimate object by the way he flinches at his own actions, as if he’s scared of what he could be capable of. She knows that feeling all too well. 

“He thinks he can fix me,” Ilya says finally, breaking the silence. “He keeps asking me how to make things okay, and I don’t know how to tell him he can’t. No one can.” 

Yuma nods, putting her own cigarette out with her shoe.  “It’s the way his brain works. If the light is green, you go. If the sky is gray, it’s going to rain. If someone has a problem, you should try and fix it. He wants to help, he just doesn’t know how.” 

Ilya sighs in resignation. “I know. I—” he stutters, pausing so that he can parse out what he wants to say next. “I wish he could help. I don’t know if anyone can. And I don’t want him to feel like there is something about him that needs to change in order for me to get better.” He inhales shakily, and pulls his knees up to his chest. “He is not what makes me so sad, and he is not what can make me okay again. I do not expect him to be, though.” 

“I know you don’t expect that from him,” Yuna says, reaching out to squeeze Ilya’s arm in comfort. “He expects it from himself, though. He loves you so much; I think he wants to make every day a good day, because he can’t understand how virtually impossible that is for people like us.” 

“Every day I am with him is good,” Ilya says weakly, resting his cheek on his kneecap. “Even when we fight. Even when he says Ilya, your socks go in the hamper, not on the floor,” he poorly mocks Shane’s accent. Yuna can’t help but laugh. “I would rather him be mad at me, than two hours away from me.” 

Yuna takes a deep breath, and reaches for him. He falls into her embrace easily, his head resting on her shoulder, like they’ve done this a million times before. Like he grew up needing the comfort she grew up so eager to give away. 

“One of these days, you’re going to have to admit that he isn’t going to leave you, just because you’re so sad you can’t move sometimes.” She feels Ilya tense in her arms, but he doesn’t pull away, so she keeps going. “You want him to realize that something about you isn’t worth keeping around, and you want him to leave sooner rather than later.” 

Ilya opens his mouth to protest, but Yuna doesn’t let him.

“You’re waiting for him to see something that isn’t visible to him, Ilya. He isn’t depressed. He can’t know what it’s like. It’s frustrating, and irritating, and it would be so much easier if he just understood, but it’s not his fault. The only thing he knows how to do is love you, and he’s terrified that he isn’t doing it right.” 

“He said I should talk to someone,” Ilya sighs, “like a therapist, maybe. I told him I do not need fixing. I’m not broken.” 

“No one thinks you are,” Yuna assures him, running a gentle hand through his hair. “There’s nothing wrong with you, Ilya. Sometimes, it just helps to talk to someone without fear of burdening them.” 

Ilya shrugs halfheartedly. “He brought up trying medication, and I said I am scared that I am like my mother.” 

Yuna inhales sharply, her breath catching as she does. She knew Ilya’s mother passed. She didn’t know the details, or how it happened. All she knows is that he was young, and that he was close with her. She’d never pry, but she knows a thing or two about complicated families, and dead mothers. 

“Tell me about her?” She asks, hoping Ilya won’t pull away. He tenses, but he stays put, his hands fidgeting with the laces on his shoes. 

“She was funny,” he starts softly, his voice thick with tears Yuna hoped he would feel safe enough to shed. “She taught me to skate, and when my father would get angry, she would take us out. To a museum, or the ballet, or the shops. Just…away.” 

Yuna presses a smile into his temple, rubbing a hand up and down his arm. 

“Everybody would always say I look just like her,” Ilya admits sadly, as if it’s something that haunts him. 

Yuna supposes it might just. 

“She made me so happy,” Ilya pauses, inhaling sharply. “But she was so sad.” 

Yuna braces herself for what she suspects is coming. 

“She did not tell anyone— especially not me and my brother. And then, one day, I came home after practice, and she did not call for me.” 

“Oh, honey—” 

“I found her,” Ilya interrupts her, his voice cracking. 

She closes her eyes, and does her best to hold back her own tears. 

“I was twelve.” 

She lets them fall. 

“Ilya,” she says, turning her head so that she can look down at him, where he’s digging his face into her neck. Ilya doesn’t respond, nor does he make any moves to meet her eyes. She feels something wet hit her collarbone, and his body shudders in her hold. “I’ve got you,” she tells him, because sometimes, that’s all you can do. 

They sit there, Ilya crying in her arms, as the sun starts to set on the horizon. Their cigarette butts sit discarded on the pavement, sad and burnt out; a sick sort of metaphor for them. 

She lets him fall apart, because she knows how badly he probably needs it. It’s become increasingly clear that Ilya’s never been allowed to just be sad. He’s spent more than half of his life denying himself that, in fear of following in his mother’s footsteps, and it’s been working. For the most part, at least. Simultaneously, it’s been weighing on him for more than a decade, and there’s only so much one can carry, before they need somewhere to put it. 

There is a sadness buried so deeply inside him, and she has a feeling she’s only just scratched the surface of it. It’s the first plate to shift before an earthquake that’s long overdue; the first puff of smoke from a volcano assumed dormant for years. There’s more to come, and she knows that things are the easiest that they ever will be, right now. That’s what scares him, she thinks. 

She remembers the first time she realized that life was a series of things getting worse, and people getting stronger. Of pain lingering, and healing taking too long. Of patience tested, and mistakes made; lessons learned, and grace given. There is a balance to life that a great sadness can make it hard to achieve, and sometimes, there is no light at the end of the tunnel— just more road to travel down. 

Right now, for Ilya, everything hurts, and nothing is getting better. It’s the most unfortunate part of healing, she knows. She won’t tell him that it will be okay, because then he’ll ask her when, and she has no real answer. She doesn’t know when, really. All she knows is that it tends to take a while for the sadness to make room for a little bit of happiness to worm its way in.   

She’ll hold him as long as he needs her to.    

—

Christmas rolls around, and Shane offers to host at his cottage. Ilya didn’t grow up celebrating Christmas on the 25th like the rest of the western hemisphere, but the MLH doesn’t schedule holiday breaks on behalf of the Russian Orthodox calendar, so they take what they can get, and head up to the cottage for the week. 

It’s a relaxing time, overall. Yuna makes a comment on the abundance of mistletoe that seems to hang in every doorway, and Shane sighs, while Ilya smirks. David cracks open the new puzzle he got, and Ilya eagerly takes over putting the edges together. Shane rolls his eyes at the matching pajama pants Yuna gets for him and Ilya, but she catches him wearing them every night that follows. 

They watch cheesy Hallmark movies about girls leaving the big city for their quaint hometowns, and Ilya makes a joke about moving from Boston to Ottawa. Shane pretends to be annoyed, and fails miserably. 

Yuna wakes on Christmas day to the usual blanket of snow covering the yard, and the sun reflecting off of it blindingly. Her son’s decision to make 70% of his cottage out of glass has her waking up earlier than usual, but with the view, she doesn’t mind it. The lake is frozen over, and the carefully coordinated lawn furniture is covered in a thick layer of fresh snow, undisturbed and serene. 

The kitchen is quiet at the early hour, and she gets to work on starting up the coffee machine. She’s just opened the fridge, cataloging the ingredients Shane has for breakfast, when she hears footsteps approaching the kitchen, and turns around to find Ilya, sleep rumpled and shirtless. 

“Yuna,” he greets, his cheeks going a bit red. “I did not think you were up.” 

“My son made a cottage out of glass. It’s hard not to wake up any later than sunrise,” she says, and Ilya cracks a smile. 

“I keep telling him is a problem for those of us with normal sleep schedules,” Ilya sighs dramatically. “He just tells me it is not normal to sleep until one.” 

“He’s always been a morning person,” she agrees, gesturing to the open fridge behind her. “Do you have any requests for breakfast?” 

Ilya’s face lights up, and he nods. “Those pancakes you made all those months ago. I meant to ask you for recipe, but I keep forgetting.” 

“Pancakes it is,” she agrees. “As long as you help me?” 

Ilya nods enthusiastically, and makes his way towards the pantry. 

They work in relative silence, resuming the roles they played the first time they did this. Ilya flips, and Yuna reminds him when to do so. Ilya plates, Yuna adds the toppings. It’s an easy routine, and by the time that they’ve finished off the batter, David and Shane are still nowhere to be seen. 

Yuna pours her and Ilya some coffee, and gestures for him to join her in the living room, taking a seat on the couch that faces the lake. Ilya curls up beside her, accepting the mug that she hands over with a small smile. 

“Merry Christmas, honey,” she says, nudging him with a gentle elbow. “I know you don’t celebrate it in Russia on the same day, but still. Merry Canadian Christmas.” 

Ilya snorts, and takes a small sip of his coffee. “Thank you,” he replies softly, staring out the window in front of them. The sun has fully risen now, but there are still remnants of the sunrise that linger, orange and pink traces staining the otherwise clear sky. 

They drink in silence, admiring the view before them, and letting the morning progress around them. Ilya finishes, but makes no move to get up. Instead, his head falls to her shoulder, settling in the junction between her chin and shoulder. Smiling to herself, she shifts so that she can wrap her arm around him, and pull him in closer. It feels reminiscent of when Shane was younger, and he would come to her for comfort after one of his bad days. 

If she’s honest, the past few months that she’s spent getting to know Ilya have made her realize that she sees him as her own, even if biology would have some kind of objection towards the sentiment. 

Biology can go fuck itself, in her opinion. Family’s never been about blood to her, anyways. 

“Everything alright?” She asks, pressing a soft kiss to Ilya’s temple. 

He shrugs against her, and looks down at where his hands are wringing in his lap. “This time of year is hard for me,” he admits quietly. 

She hums in agreement, squeezing his side in comfort. “Talk to me,” she prompts him, careful not to sound too commanding. She doesn’t want him to feel forced— just allowed. 

“My mama died in the winter,” Ilya says quietly, “and then, even after the snow stopped falling, the days were still shorter, and they never stopped getting darker. And it never stopped being cold.”

Yuna feels a pang in her chest, but she doesn’t interrupt. She suspects that what he needs right now is to say something he’s been holding in for a while now, so she lets him talk. 

“Sometimes, I dream about her, and it feels so real that it makes waking up even harder. And I tell her every time; wait for me, mama, wait because there is someone you have to meet.” Ilya swallows hard, tears shining in his eyes. “And every time, she cannot hear me, and I have taken too long. So, during this time of year, when everyone has a family to be with, it is harder. When mama died, I was only twelve.” Yuna knew that already, but the reminder still feels a bit like someone’s shoved a stake through her heart.

“I was left with only papa and Alexei, and they did not care as much as I did, because somehow they moved on, and I was just…alone.” Ilya’s voice cracks, and Yuna pulls him in as close as possible. 

“Oh, Ilya,” she sighs, her arms wrapped tightly around his figure. “You are so much stronger than I think you realize.” 

She feels him start to shake his head, but she cuts him off when he opens his mouth to speak. 

“You have carried so much, for so long, and so far. It’s okay to need a break. Anywhere that I am is a safe place for you to rest. Always.” Ilya nods slowly, his curls brushing against her chin. 

“This time of year is quite lonely, especially when you’ve lost someone,” she validates him. “After a loss, time doesn’t wait for you to heal, or move on. If it did, it would be waiting forever.”

“I don’t know how to move on,” Ilya confesses, his voice wet and laced with fear. Fear that he won’t get better, fear that he won’t be okay, fear that it will be like this forever. Yuna knows the feeling well. 

“You won’t,” she says solemnly. “When you love someone, and they leave you, that hurt doesn’t fade over time. That’s just not how it works. All that hurt is only a reminder of how much love you had, and will always have, for them, and you can never really move on from that.” 

“Everyone else did,” he protests, sounding almost angry at himself for being unable to.

“Then they did not love her, and that is the loss that they suffer.” 

Ilya turns his entire body into her side, and muffles a sob in her neck. She brings a hand up to run her fingers through his hair, and feels him melt into her. He’s the same age as Shane, but right now, the Ilya that is crying on her couch on Christmas morning, is the same Ilya that found his mother, and resigned himself to a lifetime alone. The twelve year old that lost his mother is searching for one in her, and she would be horrible not to hold him through it.

“You know what I like about this time of year?” She asks softly, tilting her head to look down at Ilya, whose sobs have quieted, but not fully subsided.

“What?” He replies, his voice barely above a whisper. 

“Even after it snows, the sun still comes out. And there’s so much snow, that the sun doesn’t melt it— it just reflects, and makes everything brighter. I like how the sun still shines in the winter.” 

“My mama used to call me her sunshine,” Ilya admits, his voice just slightly more stable than before. 

Yuna hums, and presses a gentle kiss to his hairline. “I completely understand why.” 

—

Shane wakes up about an hour later, entering the living room still half-asleep. He rubs his eyes as he makes his way over to Ilya, who’s now laying with his head on Yuna’s thigh. 

Without Shane having to say anything, Ilya opens his arms, and lets him climb onto the couch next to him. Shane shoves his head into Ilya’s neck, and Ilya pushes his nose into Shane’s hair, and Yuna can’t look away. 

She thinks about everything she’s learned about Ilya in the short time she’s known him, and how much she still wants to know. She thinks about all the things about him that make him fit so perfectly into their little family, and how grateful she is that Shane chose Ilya. 

Ilya’s mother was right to call him her sunshine. A world without him would be much darker. 

Yuna’s extremely glad that the sun still shines in the winter.
